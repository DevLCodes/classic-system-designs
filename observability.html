Monitoring, logging and tracing

How you find out a system is broken before users tell you, and then find out why: metrics, logs, traces, SLOs and alerts that fire on what users feel.

Three signals

Metrics tell you that something is wrong, traces tell you where, and logs tell you why. Each is cheap in a different way, so real systems collect all three and link them by a shared request ID.

SignalWhat it isCost driverBest for
MetricsNumbers aggregated over time: counters, gauges, histogramsNumber of distinct label combinations (cardinality), not trafficDashboards, alerts, trends, capacity
LogsOne record per event, ideally structured JSONBytes written: grows with trafficThe exact error, the input that caused it, audit trails
TracesA tree of timed spans for one request across servicesSpans kept: controlled by samplingWhere the time went; which service failed first

Monitoring answers questions you thought of in advance ("is the error rate above 1%?"). Observability is the wider goal of answering questions you did not think of, from the data you already collect. Both depend on the same three signals.

Metrics: RED and USE

Two short checklists cover most of what to measure. Use RED for anything that serves requests and USE for anything that is a resource.

MethodMeasureApplies toExample
REDRate, Errors, DurationServices, endpoints, queues' consumers2,400 req/s, 0.3% 5xx, p99 180 ms
USEUtilisation, Saturation, ErrorsCPU, memory, disks, network, connection poolsCPU 70%, run queue 4, 0 disk errors
Four golden signalsLatency, traffic, errors, saturationGoogle's SRE version; RED plus saturationAdd "DB pool 95% used" to the RED set

Percentiles, not averages

An average hides the slow tail. If 99 requests take 50 ms and one takes 5 s, the mean is about 100 ms and looks fine, but one user in a hundred waited five seconds. Track p50, p95 and p99. A page that makes 20 backend calls hits each one's p99 on about 18% of loads (1 − 0.9920), so the tail of each part becomes the typical case of the whole.

Store latency as a histogram (counts per bucket), not as pre-computed percentiles. You can add histograms from 50 servers and then take the p99; you cannot average 50 servers' p99 values and get a correct answer.

Cardinality

Every distinct set of labels is its own time series. http_requests{service, endpoint, status} with 20 endpoints and 6 status codes across 200 pods is 24,000 series per service, which is fine. Add a user_id label with a million users and it becomes billions, which will take down the metrics database. Keep unbounded values (user IDs, request IDs, full URLs) in logs and traces, not in metric labels.

SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

An SLI is a ratio you measure from the user's side: good events over all events, such as requests that succeed in under 300 ms. An SLO is the target for that ratio over a window, usually 28 or 30 days. The error budget is what is left: 1 − SLO. The requirements page covers how to pick the target; this page is about watching it.

SLOError budgetDowntime in 30 daysBad requests per 10M
99%1%7 h 12 min100,000
99.5%0.5%3 h 36 min50,000
99.9%0.1%43 min 12 s10,000
99.95%0.05%21 min 36 s5,000
99.99%0.01%4 min 19 s1,000

The budget turns reliability into a shared rule rather than an argument. While budget remains, the team ships features and runs experiments. When it is spent, the team slows releases and fixes reliability until the window rolls forward.

burn rate = observed error rate ÷ (1 − SLO)
time to empty a 30-day budget = 30 days ÷ burn rate

A burn rate of 1 spends the budget exactly by the end of the window. A burn rate of 10 spends it in 3 days. That single number is what good alerts are built on.

Try it: burn rate alerts

An incident starts and errors jump to a steady rate. Pick the SLO, how bad the incident is, and how long it has lasted. The rules are the multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts from Google's SRE workbook: each fires only when a long window and a short window both burn faster than its threshold.

SLO
RuleLong / short windowBurn ≥Fires afterNow

Assumes a 30-day window, a steady error rate from the start of the incident and zero errors before it. A rule reaches its threshold in the long window after threshold × window ÷ burn rate, so each rule always fires after spending the same share of budget: 2%, 5% or 10%.

The log pipeline

Each service writes logs to stdout. An agent on every host (Fluent Bit, Vector, the OTel collector) tails them, adds labels such as service and pod, and ships them in batches. A Kafka topic in the middle absorbs bursts: when an incident makes every service log ten times more, the buffer grows instead of the log store falling over. Indexers read from Kafka into the searchable store, and older data moves to cheap object storage.

Size it before you build it

50,000 req/s × 10 lines × 500 bytes = 250 MB/s
250 MB/s × 86,400 s ≈ 21.6 TB a day raw, about 2 TB compressed

Logs are often the biggest observability bill, and the bill grows with traffic. The usual levers: log at INFO in production and DEBUG only when switched on for one service; keep every error but sample success logs (say 1 in 100); drop health-check noise at the agent; and keep only a week hot.

Good log lines

Write structured logs (JSON with fixed field names) so you can filter by status=500 AND service=checkout instead of grepping text. Put the trace_id in every line. Never log passwords, tokens, card numbers or other personal data; redact at the agent as a second line of defence. Use UTC timestamps.

Logging must never block a request. The agent buffers to local disk and drops the oldest data if the pipeline is down for long. A checkout that fails because the log store is slow is a much worse outcome than a gap in the logs.

Distributed tracing

A trace is one request's journey. It is made of spans: one per unit of work (an HTTP call, a database query, a cache lookup), each with a start time, a duration, a parent span and tags. Drawn as a waterfall, it shows at a glance which call was slow and which ran in sequence when it could have run in parallel.

Context propagation

For spans from different services to join into one trace, every call must carry the trace ID and the parent span ID. Over HTTP and gRPC that is the W3C traceparent header. Through a queue, put the same values in the message headers, so the consumer's span links back to the producer even if it runs an hour later. One service that drops the header breaks the trace into two pieces, which is why tracing is usually done by a shared library (OpenTelemetry) rather than by hand.

Sampling

ApproachHow it worksProsCons
Head samplingThe first service decides (say 1%) and passes the decision downstream in the headerCheap; the whole trace is kept or dropped togetherDecides before knowing the result, so it keeps 1% of errors too
Tail samplingA collector buffers all spans of a trace for a few seconds, then decidesKeep every error and every slow trace, plus a small share of normal onesThe collector must hold all spans in memory and route a trace's spans to one node

A common setup: tail-sample to keep 100% of traces with an error or above the p99 latency, and 1% of the rest. Metrics still count every request, so dashboards stay exact even though most traces are thrown away.

Alerting on symptoms

Page a human for what users feel: errors and slowness on the SLI. Do not page for causes like "CPU is at 90%". High CPU with happy users is fine, and an outage with normal CPU (a bad config, an expired certificate) would never trigger it. Cause metrics belong on dashboards and in tickets, where they help explain a symptom alert.

SeverityBurn rateLong / short windowBudget spent when it firesAction
Page14.41 h / 5 min2%Wake someone now
Page66 h / 30 min5%Wake someone now
Ticket13 d / 6 h10%Fix in working hours

The long window stops a brief blip from paging. The short window makes the alert stop soon after the problem is fixed, instead of staying red for the rest of the hour. Compare a plain threshold like "error rate above 1% for 5 minutes": for a 99.9% SLO that pages on a five-minute spike to 2% errors, which used only about 0.2% of the month's budget, yet it never fires for a steady 0.5% error rate that empties the budget in 6 days.

Every page should be actionable

Each alert links to a runbook and a dashboard. If an alert fires and the right response is "wait and see", turn it into a ticket or delete it. Teams that get more than a couple of pages per on-call shift start ignoring them, and the real one gets missed.

How observability fails

Cardinality explosion

Someone adds a label with unbounded values and the metrics database runs out of memory, usually during the incident you needed it for. Set per-metric series limits and reject new series past them.

The monitor shares the outage

If monitoring runs in the same region and cluster as the product, a region outage takes out both, and the dashboards go blank at the worst moment. Run at least a minimal probe and alert path somewhere else, and add a dead man's switch: an alert that is always firing, so the pager provider pages you if it stops hearing it.

Silence looks like health

A service that crashes stops reporting errors, so its error rate drops to zero. Alert on missing data (absent() in Prometheus) and on synthetic probes that call the real endpoint from outside every minute.

Logging takes the system down

A retry loop logs a stack trace a thousand times a second, fills the disk or saturates the network, and the service falls over. Rate-limit repeated log lines, cap the agent's disk buffer, and make the log call non-blocking.

Broken or misleading traces

One hop without propagation splits traces. Clock skew between hosts makes a child span appear to start before its parent; trace UIs correct small skews, but keep NTP healthy. Head sampling at 1% means you may have no trace at all for a rare error, which is the argument for tail sampling.

Alert fatigue

Dozens of flapping cause-based alerts teach people to ignore the pager. Start from a handful of SLO burn alerts per service and add more only after an incident shows a gap.

Saying it in an interview

"Every service exports RED metrics as Prometheus histograms, logs structured JSON with the trace ID to stdout, and creates spans with OpenTelemetry, propagating traceparent over HTTP and through Kafka headers. Logs go agent to Kafka to the log store, seven days hot and then object storage. The collector tail-samples, keeping all errors and slow traces and 1% of the rest. The SLO is 99.9% of checkout requests succeeding in under 500 ms over 30 days, which is about 43 minutes of budget. I'd page on a burn rate of 14.4 over one hour and 5 minutes, and 6 over six hours and 30 minutes, ticket on a slow burn, and keep CPU and queue depth on dashboards rather than the pager. Monitoring runs outside the main region, with a dead man's switch."
