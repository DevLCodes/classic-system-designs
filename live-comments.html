Live Comments

Design live comments for posts and live videos, as on Facebook Live or YouTube Live: when someone comments, everyone currently watching that post sees it within seconds. Every minute about 1 million posts and streams are being watched by 50 million people, who write 650,000 comments, and 60% of those land on the 10,000 hottest streams.

Requirements

  • Clarify
  • Non-functional requirements

The write rate is modest. The hard parts are knowing who is watching what as people scroll, and fanning comments out to huge audiences concentrated on a few hot streams.

Estimates

  • Back of the envelope
  • Numbers
  • Writes: 650k ÷ 60 ≈ 11k comments a second. At ~1 KB with metadata, about 11 MB/s or 40 GB an hour. (Notes that say 650 GB an hour are off by about 16×.) One modest Kafka cluster with 100 partitions handles it at ~110 messages a second each.
  • Connections: 50M viewers at ~100k connections per gateway server ≈ 500 gateways, each with a few GB of RAM for connection state.
  • Subscription churn: people scroll past posts every few seconds. If each viewer changes what they watch every ~100 s, that is 500k subscribe or unsubscribe events a second. This, not comments, is the busiest write stream.
  • Fan-out: a hot stream with 1M viewers and 200 comments a second would be 200M pushes a second if sent naively. Capping each viewer at 10 comments a second, batched once a second, makes it 1M small pushes a second for that stream.

Submitting is a normal request, so it gets retries, auth and rate limits for free; a client-generated id makes retries safe. Receiving is a single long-lived connection per client that multiplexes every stream on screen. gRPC with protobuf is a good choice between our own services and for native apps; browsers use HTTPS or the WebSocket itself.

Architecture

  • Whole system
  • Comment service: stateless; checks auth, rate limits and content filters, assigns an id, and appends to Kafka keyed by stream_id, so a stream's comments stay in order on one partition.
  • Storage consumer: writes comments to Cassandra for history. Kafka keeps them if the database is slow.
  • Fan-out workers: consume Kafka, decide what to deliver (everything on quiet streams, a ranked sample on busy ones), look up which gateways care, and send.
  • Gateways: hold WebSocket connections, keep a local map stream → my connections, and keep the registry up to date as viewers watch and unwatch.

Push, not poll

  • Deep dive 1
  • Polling vs push
  • Live stock prices

Facebook first tried polling for new comments and dropped it: to feel live, every page would need to poll every few seconds, and the request rate would dwarf the actual comments.

With 50M viewers polling every 5 seconds, that is 10M requests a second, nearly all answering "nothing new", and comments still arrive 2.5 seconds late on average. Pushing over an open connection sends only real updates, as soon as they exist. The cost is state: someone must hold 50M connections and know which ones care about each stream.

OptionFit
Short pollingSimple and cacheable, but wasteful and laggy at this scale; fine for a page with a few comments a day
Server-Sent EventsOne-way server push over HTTP with automatic reconnect; enough for receiving comments
WebSocketTwo-way; lets watch and unwatch messages travel on the same connection. The usual choice

Batch what you push: one message a second per stream carrying several comments costs far less than one message per comment, and viewers can't tell the difference.

Who is watching what

  • Deep dive 2
  • Sharding

The registry is a fast-changing map from stream to audience. How you key it decides whether it melts under hot streams.

  1. Key by gateway, not user. Storing stream → [user ids] means millions of entries for a hot stream and an update on every scroll. Storing stream → {gateways} means at most ~500 entries per stream, and a gateway only updates the registry when its first viewer of a stream arrives or its last one leaves. Most scrolling stays inside the gateway.
  2. Prefer composite keys to big lists. If you do keep per-user data, store (stream_id, gateway_id) as separate keys and read them with a prefix scan, rather than one big list value that must be locked and rewritten on every change.
  3. Expire, don't trust. Every entry has a TTL of a minute or two, refreshed by the gateway while it still has viewers. A crashed gateway's entries vanish by themselves, so the fan-out stops sending to it.
  4. Shard by stream id. The registry (Redis or a similar store) is partitioned by stream_id, so a lookup touches one shard. Gateways are found by id, so delivery is "send to gateway 17", not a broadcast.

Write locally, read globally. Each region keeps its own registry and gateways. A comment is published to every region's Kafka (or replicated with MirrorMaker), and each region fans out to its own viewers. No cross-region lookups on the delivery path.

Hot streams

  • Deep dive 3
  • Caching

10,000 streams carry 60% of comments, and a single World Cup stream can have millions of viewers. Everything keyed by stream id becomes a hot key.

TechniqueWhat it fixesCost
Rank and sample to ~10 comments a second per stream (friends first, then quality score)Fan-out volume and readabilityMost comments on huge streams are never shown live (still stored)
Batch once a second per stream per gatewayMessage countUp to a second of extra delay
Sub-shard the hot key: s_91#0 … s_91#15 for registry entries and Kafka keysOne shard or partition meltingReaders must merge the sub-keys
Separate lane: dedicated partitions and workers for streams above a thresholdNoisy hot streams delaying quiet onesDetection and migration logic
Relay tree: fan-out sends to regional relays, which send to their gatewaysFan-out of one message to hundreds of gatewaysAn extra hop
Coalesce registry updates per hot streamSubscription churn on one keyRegistry is a few seconds stale

Replication alone doesn't fix a hot registry key, because the problem is write volume: every replica still takes every write. Coalescing and sub-sharding do.

Storage

  • Cassandra
comments_by_stream
  stream_id   text         -- partition key (plus a day bucket for long streams)
  comment_id  timeuuid     -- clustering key, newest first
  user_id, text, status (visible | hidden | deleted), score

Comments are append-only, read newest-first by stream, and written at a steady 11k a second with spikes on hot streams. That is the shape Cassandra or ScyllaDB is built for: leaderless, so there is no primary to fail over, and writes scale by adding nodes. Write at LOCAL_QUORUM. A document database with a single primary per shard works at lower volume, but under hot-stream spikes its primary becomes the bottleneck and an election blocks writes.

Kafka keeps comments for a day, so a slow or failed storage consumer catches up without losing anything, and a new consumer (search, analytics, moderation models) can replay recent history.

Moderation

  • Governance
  • Rate limiting
  • Before fan-out: per-user and per-stream rate limits, blocklists, and a fast classifier for spam and slurs. Anything flagged is stored but not delivered.
  • After fan-out: a hide or delete sends a tombstone message on the same path, so clients remove the comment already on screen.
  • Creator controls: slow mode (one comment per user per N seconds), followers-only, keyword filters, all enforced in the comment service.

Deployment and failure

  • Production
  • Gateway dies: its 100k clients reconnect to other gateways with jittered backoff, re-send their watch list, and fetch missed comments with ?after=last_seen_id. The dead gateway's registry entries expire. Drain gateways gracefully before deploys so reconnects are spread out.
  • Registry shard lost: rebuild from gateways, which re-announce their streams; until then, fan-out for those streams falls back to broadcasting to all gateways in the region, which costs more but delivers.
  • Fan-out lagging: drop the oldest live deliveries first. A comment delivered 30 seconds late is worse than one shown only in history.
  • Kafka down: the comment service writes straight to Cassandra and returns success; live delivery pauses, history is intact.

"Writes are only 11k a second; the challenge is fan-out. Clients keep one WebSocket to a gateway and send watch and unwatch as they scroll. Gateways register 'I have viewers of stream X' in a registry sharded by stream, with TTLs. A comment goes through the comment service to Kafka keyed by stream and to Cassandra; fan-out workers look up the gateways for that stream and send one copy to each, and each gateway pushes to its local viewers. Hot streams get ranking down to about 10 comments a second, one batch a second, sub-sharded keys and their own lane, so fan-out is bounded by gateways, not comments."
