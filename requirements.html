Non-functional requirements

Functional requirements say what the system does. Non-functional requirements say how well: how fast, how big, how available, how safe, how cheap. They decide the architecture, so pin them down with numbers before drawing any boxes.

Functional vs non-functional

"Users can post a photo" is functional. "A posted photo appears in followers' feeds within 5 seconds, for 200 million daily users, at 99.95% availability" is non-functional, and it is the part that tells you whether you need a cache, a queue, sharding or a second region.

You won't need every row for every problem. Pick the three or four that will shape the design (usually scale, latency, availability and consistency), get numbers for them, and state the rest as assumptions.

Turning words into numbers

SLIs, SLOs and SLAs

An SLI (indicator) is something you measure: the share of requests that succeed in under 300 ms. An SLO (objective) is the target for it: 99.9% over 30 days. An SLA (agreement) is a contract with consequences, usually looser than the SLO so you have room. The gap between 100% and the SLO is the error budget: at 99.9%, about 43 minutes of failure a month that you may spend on deploys, experiments and bad luck.

Percentiles, not averages

An average latency of 80 ms can hide a p99 of 2 seconds. A page that makes 50 backend calls hits the p99 of at least one of them on most loads (1 − 0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 40%), so tail latency is what users feel. State latency targets as percentiles per operation.

RTO and RPO

RPO = how much recent data you may lose · RTO = how long until service is back

An RPO of zero needs synchronous replication to another zone or region before acknowledging a write (slower writes). An RPO of 5 minutes can be met with asynchronous replication or frequent log shipping. An RTO of minutes needs a warm standby that can take traffic; an RTO of hours can be met by restoring from backups. Test restores; an untested backup is a hope, not a plan.

Common mistakes

  • Adjectives instead of numbers. "Highly available and scalable" decides nothing. "99.95%, 50k reads/s at peak" does.
  • One consistency level for everything. Most systems need strong consistency in one or two places and eventual everywhere else.
  • Forgetting the read/write ratio. A 100:1 read-heavy system and a write-heavy event pipeline need opposite designs.
  • Five nines by default. Each extra nine costs roughly ten times as much effort. Ask what downtime actually costs the business.
  • Ignoring peaks. Size for the evening peak or the sale day (3 to 10× average), not the daily mean.

Saying it in an interview

"Before designing, let me pin down the non-functional side. I'll assume 50 million daily users and a 20:1 read-to-write ratio, so roughly 25k reads a second at peak. Reads should be under 200 ms at p99. Availability target 99.95%, which is about 22 minutes a month. Posts must never be lost once acknowledged, but feeds can be a few seconds stale, so I'll take eventual consistency there. Data stays in-region for EU users. Does that match what you had in mind?"
