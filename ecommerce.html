Classic system design walkthrough

E-commerce Store

Design an online store like Amazon or Flipkart: customers search and browse products, keep a cart and a wishlist, check out, and view their orders. Browsing must be fast and always up; checkout must never sell stock that doesn't exist or charge someone twice. Plan for 100 million daily shoppers and sale days with 10× normal traffic.

Requirements

  • Clarify
  • Non-functional requirements

An e-commerce site is two systems with opposite needs. Browsing wants low latency and high availability and can be slightly stale. Checkout wants strong consistency for stock, orders and money.

Estimates

  • Back of the envelope
  • Numbers
  • Browsing: 100M shoppers × ~30 page views = 3B a day ≈ 35k a second, and 10× on sale days. Most of it can be served from CDN and caches.
  • Orders: 10M a day ≈ 120 a second. Sale peaks are 10× on average, but a single "deal of the hour" can draw thousands of attempts a second on one product.
  • Catalog: 500M products × ~5 KB of attributes ≈ 2.5 TB, plus images in object storage behind the CDN.
  • Orders storage: 3.6B orders a year × ~2 KB with line items ≈ 7 TB a year: sharded, but not big.

Product pages are split: the slow-changing description is cached for minutes at the CDN, while price and stock come from a tiny "offer" call cached for seconds. Checkout takes an idempotency key, so a double click or a retry after a timeout never creates two orders.

Architecture

  • Whole system

Catalog and search

  • Deep dive 1
  • Caching
  • Catalog service: product attributes in a document store or sharded relational database, keyed by product id, with Redis in front and rendered pages at the CDN. A product page fans out to catalog, offers, reviews and recommendations in parallel, with timeouts; a slow reviews service shows the page without reviews, not an error.
  • Search: Elasticsearch or OpenSearch with the catalog denormalized into it: title, brand, category, attributes for facets, price and an "in stock" flag. It is updated from catalog and inventory change events, seconds behind, which is fine for browsing.
  • Offers: price and availability change often and differ by seller and region, so they are a separate small service with a short cache. The number shown on the page is a hint; the real check happens at checkout.

Cart and wishlist

  • Key-value stores

The cart is per user, read and written by key, and must always accept "add to cart", even during partial failures. Amazon built Dynamo for exactly this: an always-writable cart where conflicting versions from different replicas are merged (union of items) rather than lost. Today a key-value store like DynamoDB or Cassandra, partitioned by user id, does the job.

The cart holds SKUs and quantities, not prices: prices are re-read at checkout. Guest carts live in the browser or under a session id and merge into the account cart on login. The wishlist is the same shape with no expiry.

Checkout and inventory

  • Deep dive 2
  • Sagas
  • Outbox
  • Payments

Checkout touches three services, each with its own database, so there is no single transaction. It is a saga: a sequence of local steps, each with a compensating step if a later one fails.

  1. Create order in PENDING, keyed by the idempotency key so a retry finds the same order.
  2. Reserve stock: UPDATE stock SET available = available − 1, reserved = reserved + 1 WHERE sku = ? AND available ≥ 1, plus a reservation row with an expiry (say 10 minutes). If no row is updated, the item is out of stock and the order fails before anyone is charged.
  3. Charge through the payment service with the order id as the idempotency key. On a timeout, the payment is unknown, not failed: query the provider before deciding.
  4. Confirm: mark the order CONFIRMED, turn the reservation into a sale, and write an OrderConfirmed event to the outbox table in the same transaction.
  5. Compensate on failure: release the reservation, refund if a charge went through, mark the order FAILED. Expired reservations are released by a sweeper, so a crashed checkout never locks stock forever.

An outbox relay publishes the event to Kafka, and fulfilment, email, analytics and search consume it. Consumers are idempotent because the relay delivers at least once.

Flash sales

  • Deep dive 3
  • Rate limiting

10,000 phones go on sale at noon and 2 million people press "buy" in the same minute. One inventory row can't take that.

  • Waiting room: a queue page in front of checkout admits buyers at the rate the back end can handle and tells everyone else their place. It also blocks most bots, with challenges and per-account limits.
  • Pre-split stock: load the 10,000 units into an in-memory counter (Redis DECR, or a Lua script that checks and decrements), sharded into, say, 20 counters of 500, so no single key is hot. A successful decrement is a token that lets the buyer proceed to a durable reservation.
  • Or queue the buys: put purchase requests for the SKU on one Kafka partition and let a single consumer allocate units in order, in memory, writing results in batches. No lock contention at all, at the cost of an asynchronous "you got one" answer.
  • Return expired holds: reservations not paid within 10 minutes go back to the counter, so stock that was "sold" to abandoned carts gets sold again.
  • Protect everything else: separate capacity (or a separate deployment) for the sale, so the rest of the store keeps working.

Try it: sell 10,000 phones

  • Lab

Choose how hard buyers hit the sale and how inventory is updated. See how fast it sells out, how many requests fail on contention, and whether anything is oversold.

Inventory

Rough model: a conditional update on one row holds a lock for ~5 ms (≈200 updates a second); sharded counters scale that by 20; a single in-memory consumer allocates ~20,000 a second in batches. "Read then write" checks stock and decrements in two steps without a condition.

Deployment

  • Production

Browse services run active-active in several regions behind the CDN. Checkout services can be active-active too, but inventory for a SKU needs a single source of truth: either a home region per warehouse or seller, or a globally consistent database. Before a known sale, scale up ahead of time (autoscaling reacts too slowly to a noon spike), pre-warm caches with the sale products, and load test the exact sale path.

Presenting it in 45 minutes

  • Interview script
StepTimeWhat to cover

7. Risks and v25 minOverselling, bots, isolation

"I'd treat browsing and checkout as different systems. Browsing is cached at the CDN and in Redis, search runs on Elasticsearch fed by change events, and the cart lives in an always-writable key-value store by user. Checkout is a saga in the order service: create a pending order under an idempotency key, reserve stock with a conditional decrement and a 10-minute expiry, charge with the order id as the payment's idempotency key, then confirm and write an outbox event in one transaction. For flash sales I'd put a waiting room in front and pre-split stock into sharded in-memory counters, so 10,000 units sell in seconds without overselling or melting one database row."
