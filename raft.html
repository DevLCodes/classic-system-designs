Consensus with Raft

How a handful of servers agree on one ordered log, even when some of them crash or the network splits. Raft is what keeps etcd, Consul, CockroachDB and Kafka's metadata correct.

Why consensus

One database server is consistent but dies. Several copies survive failures but can disagree. Consensus gives you both: a group of servers that acts like one, as long as a majority is up and can talk.

The trick is a replicated log. Every change ("set x = 5", "move range 12 to node 3") is appended to a log, the servers agree on the order of entries, and each server applies them in that order to its own copy of the state (a state machine). Same log, same order, same state. Raft (2014) was designed to be easier to understand and implement than Paxos, and it is now the default choice.

You rarely implement it. You use it through a system built on it when you need a small amount of state that must never be wrong: who the leader is, which node owns which shard, a lock, the cluster's config.

Leaders, followers and terms

  • Leader. One per term. Clients send writes to it; it appends them to its log, sends them to followers and sends heartbeats so followers know it's alive.
  • Followers. Passive: they accept entries from the leader and vote in elections.
  • Terms. A counter that only goes up. Every message carries the sender's term; anyone who sees a higher term updates to it, and a leader that sees one steps down. Terms are how stale leaders are detected without clocks.

Leader election

  1. A follower hears nothing from a leader for its election timeout, randomly chosen between about 150 and 300 ms.
  2. It becomes a candidate, increments the term, votes for itself and asks every other server for a vote.
  3. Each server grants at most one vote per term, and only to a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own (higher last term, or same term and at least as long). This rule ensures a new leader already has every committed entry.
  4. With votes from a majority, the candidate becomes leader and starts sending heartbeats.

The random timeouts make it unlikely that two followers start an election at the same moment and split the vote; if they do, both time out again at different random moments and one wins.

Log replication

  1. The leader appends the client's command to its log as entry (index, term).
  2. It sends AppendEntries to every follower, including the index and term of the entry just before the new ones.
  3. A follower accepts only if its log has that previous entry. If not, it refuses, and the leader backs up and resends earlier entries until the logs match. This consistency check is how a lagging or recovering follower is repaired.
  4. Once a majority (including the leader) has the entry, it is committed. The leader applies it to its state machine, replies to the client, and tells followers the new commit index in later messages.

Logs grow forever, so servers periodically write a snapshot of the state and discard the log before it; a follower that is far behind gets the snapshot instead of millions of entries. Adding or removing servers goes through the log too, one server at a time, so two different majorities can never exist.

Try it: break a cluster

A five-server Raft cluster. Write entries, kill the leader, split the network, then heal it. Watch which writes commit, who leads, and what happens to writes accepted by a leader that lost its majority.

A teaching model, not a full implementation: elections happen instantly and pick the most up-to-date reachable server. Green entries are committed; grey entries are stored but not committed.

What it costs

Cluster sizeMajorityToleratesNotes
321 failureThe usual choice
532 failuresSurvive one failure during maintenance of another
4 or 63 or 4Same as 3 or 5Even sizes add cost without tolerance
7+4+3+Rare: every write waits on more servers
write latency ≈ leader's disk fsync + round trip to the slowest server in the fastest majority

In one region that is a millisecond or two. Spread across three regions it is a cross-region round trip, 30 to 100 ms, on every write; that is the price of surviving a region loss. Reads served by the leader must also confirm it is still leader (a heartbeat round, or a time-based lease), or a deposed leader could serve stale data. All writes go through one leader, so a single Raft group is limited to what one server can handle; systems like CockroachDB and TiKV run thousands of small Raft groups, one per data range, to scale.

Failure modes

Split brain, prevented

In a 2 | 3 split, the side with 3 can elect a leader and commit; the side with 2 cannot reach a majority, so its old leader can accept writes but never commit them. When the network heals, the old leader sees a higher term, steps down, and its uncommitted entries are overwritten. Clients that got no success reply must retry, which is why requests should be idempotent.

Leader flapping

Election timeouts that are too short relative to network or disk latency (a GC pause, a slow fsync) cause needless elections, each a brief write outage. Tune timeouts to well above your p99 heartbeat delay; Raft implementations add "pre-vote" so a flaky node can't keep disrupting a healthy leader.

Lost majority

Lose 3 of 5 and the cluster stops accepting writes, by design. Recovery from permanent loss needs a manual, carefully documented procedure; practice it.

Slow disks

Every commit waits on an fsync. Put the log on fast, dedicated SSDs; etcd warns when fsync takes more than about 10 ms.

Saying it in an interview

"Shard ownership and leader election live in etcd, which uses Raft: three or five nodes, one leader per term, writes committed once a majority has them, and an up-to-date-log rule in elections so a new leader never loses committed data. Three nodes survive one failure; I'd use five across three zones to survive a zone loss during maintenance. Writes cost a quorum round trip, so I keep only small, critical metadata there, not user data. A minority partition can't commit, so there's no split brain; clients retry idempotently after a leader change."
