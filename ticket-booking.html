Classic system design walkthrough

Ticket Booking

Design a movie and event ticketing site like BookMyShow or Ticketmaster: users find a show, see a live seat map, pick seats, pay, and get a ticket. Two people must never get the same seat, and a seat someone abandons must come back for sale. Plan for 50 million daily users, about a million bookings a day, and blockbuster releases where a million people arrive in the first minute for 50,000 seats.

Requirements

Ticketing is an inventory problem where every unit is unique. A phone is a phone; seat F7 at the 7 pm show is one specific thing that exactly one person may own.

Estimates

  • Back of the envelope
  • Numbers
  • Browsing: 50M users × ~10 seat map or show views ≈ 500M a day ≈ 6k a second, with release-day spikes of 100k+ a second on a handful of shows.
  • Bookings: 1M a day ≈ 12 a second on average, maybe 10× in the evening. Tiny for one database, except when they all target the same show.
  • Seat inventory: 250k shows × 200 seats = 50M seat rows a day. Keep only upcoming shows hot (say 2 weeks, ~700M rows) and archive the rest.
  • Hot release: 1M people for 50k seats means 95% of them leave without tickets. The job is to tell them quickly and fairly, not to serve them all.

The seat map is served from a cache that can be a second or two stale; the hold call is the real check. A hold either gets all requested seats or none, so a family is never split across a row they didn't choose.

Architecture

  • Whole system

Shows and seat maps

  • Deep dive 1
  • Caching

Most traffic is people looking, not buying. Serve it without touching the booking database.

  • Catalog: movies, venues, screens and show times change rarely. Store them in Postgres, index them in Elasticsearch for "movies near me tonight", and cache responses at the CDN for minutes.
  • Layout vs state: a screen's layout (rows, aisles, tiers, prices) is static and versioned, so it is cached forever by layout_version. Only the state of each seat changes.
  • Seat state as a bitmap: 200 seats × 2 bits (free, held, sold) is 50 bytes per show. Keep it in Redis, update it from hold and booking events, and let clients poll it every few seconds or subscribe over a WebSocket during a hot release.
  • Stale is fine here: a seat that looks free but was just taken costs the user one "sorry, pick another" from the hold call. That is much cheaper than making 100k viewers a second read the source of truth.

Seat holds

  • Deep dive 2
  • Consistency
  • Databases

Paying takes minutes. Without a hold, two people pay for F7 and one of them gets a refund and an angry email. With a hold, the first to click gets F7 for 10 minutes and everyone else sees it as taken.

Three ways to place a hold, from simplest to most scalable:

OptionHowTrade-off
Database row lockSELECT … FOR UPDATE on the seat rows, then update to HELDCorrect but holds locks while users dither; never keep a lock open across a payment page
Conditional updateUPDATE seats SET state='HELD', hold_id=?, expires_at=now()+10m WHERE show_id=? AND seat_id IN (…) AND (state='FREE' OR expires_at < now()); succeed only if every row changedCorrect, no long locks; needs a sweeper (or the expires_at check) to free old holds
Redis key per seatSET hold:{show}:{seat} {hold_id} NX EX 600 for each seat in a Lua script, all or nothingFastest, expires by itself; Redis is not the system of record, so the database still has the final say

Whichever you pick, the booking is finally written to a table with UNIQUE (show_id, seat_id). If a Redis failover ever lets two holds through, the second insert fails and that payment is refunded: an annoyance, never a double sale.

Try it: tune the hold timeout

  • Lab

A stadium show with 50,000 seats opens for sale. Set how fast the waiting room admits buyers, how long a hold lasts, and how many buyers abandon at the payment page. Little's law (seats on hold = holds started per minute × minutes each hold lasts) shows how many seats are locked away at any moment.

Hold timeout

Rough model: each buyer holds 2.5 seats; buyers who pay take between 2 and 6 minutes (uniform), and fail if the hold expires first; abandoners keep their seats until the hold expires.

Booking and payment

  • Deep dive 3
  • Payments
  • Outbox
  1. Start payment: create a booking in PENDING_PAYMENT tied to the hold, and charge through the payment service with the booking id as the idempotency key.
  2. Confirm: on the payment webhook, in one transaction insert the booked seats (the unique key guards them), mark the booking CONFIRMED, and write a SeatsBooked event to the outbox. Then delete the Redis hold.
  3. Late payment: if the hold expired and someone else booked the seat, the insert fails. Mark the booking EXPIRED and refund automatically. Extending the hold by a minute or two while a payment is in flight makes this rare.
  4. Unknown payment: a timeout from the provider is not a failure. Keep the hold, ask the provider, and decide; a reconciliation job checks every booking stuck in PENDING_PAYMENT.
  5. After booking: consumers of the event issue the QR ticket, send email and SMS, update the seat bitmap, and tell the venue's box office system.

Blockbuster releases

  • Deep dive 4
  • Rate limiting
  • Flash sales
  • Waiting room: everyone who arrives before opening gets a random place in line (fairer than "first to refresh"); after that, first come first served. The room admits buyers at the rate the hold service can take and shows everyone else their position and wait.
  • Sold-out fast path: once available + held seats reach zero, the waiting room says "sold out, join the waitlist" without letting anyone further in.
  • Partition by show: holds and bookings are sharded by show_id, so one hot show lives on one shard and can't slow down the other 250k shows. Give known blockbusters their own capacity.
  • Limits and bots: a maximum number of seats per account and per card, verified accounts or a presale code for the hottest events, and challenges for suspicious traffic.
  • Best available: for stadiums, let users pick a tier and quantity and have the server pick adjacent seats. Fewer people fight over the same seat, and holds succeed on the first try.

Variant: restaurant reservations

  • Interview twist

"A table for four at a grill around 6 pm today; show me 6:00, 6:30 and 7:30." Same shape, different inventory.

  • Inventory is slots, not seats: each restaurant has tables (2-, 4-, 6-tops) and a seating length (say 90 minutes for four). A slot is free if some table that fits the party is free for that whole window. Precompute availability per restaurant per day into 15-minute buckets and cache it.
  • Search: "around 6 pm" becomes a window (5:00–7:30), and the service returns the free slots nearest to 6. Across many restaurants this is a geo plus availability query, like Nearby places with a time filter.
  • Booking: a conditional insert into reservations(table_id, start, end) that fails if it overlaps an existing one (an exclusion constraint in Postgres does exactly this). No payment step, so a short hold of a minute or two is enough, or none at all.
  • Different risks: no-shows instead of bots. Card guarantees, reminders and an easy cancel matter more than a waiting room. Scale is small: even a big platform does a few hundred bookings a second, so one sharded Postgres by restaurant is plenty.
  • MVP for a startup CTO: one web app, one Postgres, Redis for cached availability, and a job queue for reminders. Split services only when traffic or teams demand it.

"Browsing and booking are separate paths. Shows and layouts are cached at the CDN, and each show's seat state is a tiny bitmap in Redis, slightly stale. When a user picks seats, the booking service places an all-or-nothing hold with SET NX and a 10-minute TTL, so the first click wins and abandoned seats come back on their own. Payment uses the booking id as its idempotency key, and confirmation inserts the seats into a Postgres table with a unique key on show and seat, sharded by show, plus an outbox event for tickets and the seat map. For blockbusters a waiting room admits buyers at the rate holds can be served and says 'sold out' the moment nothing is left."
