Classic system design walkthrough

Stock Trading Platform

Design the trading side of a retail stockbroker like Zerodha Kite or Robinhood: users watch live prices, place and cancel orders, and see their positions, holdings and funds. Orders go to the stock exchange, which does the matching. Plan for 15 million orders a day, 2 million people connected at the 9:15 market open, and live prices on every phone. An order must never be lost, sent twice, or placed without the money to cover it.

Requirements

  • Clarify
  • Non-functional requirements

A broker is a careful middleman. It does not decide prices or match buyers with sellers; the exchange does that. The broker checks each order, sends it to the exchange, and keeps the books for its users.

QuestionAssumptionWhy it matters
FeaturesWatchlists with live prices; place, modify, cancel orders; order book, positions, holdings, fundsOrder path and market data path are separate systems
Users15M accounts, 6M active on a busy dayAccounts, sessions, margins
Orders~15M a dayOrder service and exchange link throughput
Market hours9:15 to 15:30, 6 h 15 minAll of the load lands in one window, with a spike at the open
Live prices2M connected at the open, ~50 symbols eachA WebSocket fan-out far bigger than the order flow
CorrectnessNo lost or duplicated orders; no order without marginIdempotency, an order state machine, pre-trade risk checks
Latencytap to exchange under ~50 ms inside the brokerRetail users feel slowness at the open more than at any other time

Out of scope: account opening and KYC, mutual funds, research and charts, and algorithmic trading APIs (they reuse the same order path). Moving money in and out uses Payments.

Estimates

  • Back of the envelope
  • Numbers
  • Orders: 15M a day over 22,500 seconds of trading is about 670 a second. If 20% of them arrive in the first 15 minutes, that is 3M in 900 seconds, about 3,300 a second.
  • The first second: orders placed overnight (after-market orders, AMOs) are all due at the open. 300k of them cannot go out in one second; the exchange caps how many orders a broker may send a second, so they go out in a queue.
  • Market data: 2M phones × 50 symbols × 1 update a second is up to 100M tick updates a second. At 44 bytes per tick that is 4.4 GB/s, about 35 Gb/s. At 50k sockets per gateway that is 40 gateways; run 80 to survive a zone loss.
  • Storage: 15M orders × ~1 KB with their state changes is about 15 GB a day, or under 4 TB a year at ~250 trading days. Regulators require years of records, so it moves to cheap storage, never deleted early.

Placing an order returns once the broker has accepted it, not once it trades. The final result comes later over the WebSocket. The idempotency key lets the app retry a tap that timed out without placing the order twice.

Architecture

  • Whole system

Order flow and risk checks

  • Deep dive 1
  • Idempotency
  • Consistency

Every order passes the same line of checks. Each one is cheap, and none of them may be skipped, even at 9:15.

  1. Gateway: checks the session token and a per-user rate limit, so one runaway script cannot flood the order path.
  2. Order service (OMS): looks up the idempotency key. A retry of an order it already has returns the same order id. A new order gets an id and is saved as RECEIVED before anything else happens.
  3. Risk checks (RMS): is the symbol tradable today, is the price inside the day's price band and on a valid tick, is the quantity under the per-order limit, and is there enough margin? For a delivery buy of 100 ACME at ₹412.50, that is the full ₹41,250. The RMS blocks that amount at once, so a second order a millisecond later sees less money.
  4. Exchange gateway: sends the order over a long-lived session to the exchange, within the broker's orders-a-second cap, and records it as SENT. The exchange replies with its own order id and OPEN, or a rejection.
  5. Fills: each trade comes back as an event. The order moves to PARTIAL or COMPLETE, blocked margin becomes used margin, the position updates, and the app gets a push.
StateMeaningNext
RECEIVEDSaved by the broker, not yet checkedVALIDATED or REJECTED
VALIDATEDPassed risk checks, margin blockedSENT
SENTOn the wire to the exchange; the result is unknownOPEN or REJECTED, never resent blindly
OPEN / PARTIALResting in the exchange's book, maybe partly filledCOMPLETE, CANCELLED, or modified
COMPLETE / CANCELLED / REJECTEDFinalMargin released or used

The dangerous state is SENT. If the exchange link drops right after sending, the broker doesn't know whether the order arrived. Resending could buy 200 shares instead of 100. Instead, the exchange gateway reconnects, replays the session from its last sequence number, and asks for the order's status. This is the same "unknown is not failed" rule as a payment timeout.

Why the RMS is partitioned by account

Margin is a shared balance that many orders spend at once. Locking a database row per order is slow at 9:15. Instead, the RMS keeps margins in memory and sends each account to one partition, where a single thread handles its orders one after another. No locks, no double spending, and each check takes microseconds. The partition writes every change to a replicated log, so a standby can take over with the exact balance.

The order book and matching

  • Deep dive 2
  • How the exchange does it

The exchange keeps one order book per symbol: buy orders (bids) from the highest price down, and sell orders (asks) from the lowest price up. A trade happens when the best bid meets the best ask.

Bids (buy)QtyAsks (sell)Qty
₹412.45300₹412.55100 + 250
₹412.40100 + 200₹412.60200
₹412.30500₹412.70150
  • Price, then time: a new buy at ₹412.60 for 200 first takes the cheapest asks. At ₹412.55 there are two orders, so the older one (100) fills first, then 100 of the newer one (250). Being first at a price is worth something.
  • The resting price wins: the buyer offered up to ₹412.60 but pays ₹412.55, the price of the order already in the book.
  • Leftovers rest: if a limit order is not fully filled, the rest joins the book at its price, behind everyone already there. A market order takes whatever is available and never rests.
  • One thread per book: the matching engine handles each symbol's orders strictly one at a time, in memory, from a sequenced input log. That makes the result deterministic: replaying the log rebuilds the exact book. Different symbols run in parallel.
  • Speed comes from simplicity: no database in the hot path, sorted price levels with a FIFO queue at each, and the log written in sequence. Matching takes microseconds; the network to reach it costs more.

In an interview, say clearly that the broker does not match orders. If you are asked to design the exchange instead, this section becomes the core: a sequencer, one matching thread per symbol, a replicated input log for failover, and market data built from the book.

Market data fan-out

  • Deep dive 3
  • Live comments
  • WebSocket gateways

The exchange publishes every change to every book. The broker must turn that firehose into a calm stream of the latest prices for each phone.

  • Ticker plant: servers close to the exchange receive its feed (often UDP multicast over leased lines), decode it, and keep the current state of every symbol in memory: last price, volume, best bids and asks.
  • Conflation: a busy symbol can change hundreds of times a second. A phone needs maybe one update a second. The plant publishes snapshots on a timer, so a hundred ticks collapse into one message. This is the single biggest saving.
  • Pub/sub by symbol: snapshots go to a pub/sub layer partitioned by symbol. Each WebSocket gateway subscribes only to symbols its own users watch, and keeps a symbol-to-sockets map, like the stream registry in Live comments.
  • Batching: each second a gateway sends each socket one binary frame with all of that user's changed symbols. 50 ticks of 44 bytes in one frame beats 50 frames with headers.
  • Slow clients: a phone on a weak network falls behind. The gateway keeps only the latest snapshot per symbol for each socket and overwrites it, so a slow phone skips stale prices instead of building a queue that eats the gateway's memory.
  • Modes: a watchlist needs only the last price (a few bytes); the order screen asks for full depth (the top 5 bids and asks). Paying for depth only where it is shown keeps the 35 Gb/s from tripling.

The 9:15 market open

  • Deep dive 4
  • Rate limiting
  • Blockbuster releases

Most systems see traffic grow over minutes. A broker sees it arrive at a known second, every weekday.

TimeWhat happensWhat the system does
Before 8:45QuietScale out to open-day capacity on a schedule; load today's instruments, bands and margins; warm caches
9:00–9:08Pre-open order entry for equitiesRelease queued AMOs in the order they were placed, within the exchange's rate cap
9:08–9:15The exchange runs a call auction to set the open price; logins surgeAbsorb millions of logins; sessions expire daily, so nearly everyone logs in again
9:15Continuous trading starts; prices jumpPeak orders, peak sockets, peak ticks, all at once
  • Scale before, not during: autoscaling reacts in minutes, and the spike lasts minutes. Capacity is booked on a schedule, sized from the busiest open of the last few months plus headroom.
  • Logins: 2M logins between 9:05 and 9:15 is about 3,300 a second, each with a password hash and a second factor. Password hashing is slow by design, so the auth service needs its own capacity plan.
  • Priority lanes: cancels and exits go ahead of new orders, because someone trying to get out of a falling stock matters most. Reports, charts and history are the first things to slow down.
  • Exchange cap: the exchange limits each broker's orders a second per session. The exchange gateway spreads orders across sessions and queues the rest, fairly by arrival time. Buying more sessions is the only way to raise the cap.
  • Fail clearly: if the order path is overloaded, reject fast with "try again", never hang. A user who doesn't know whether their order went through will tap again.

Holdings and settlement

  • After the trade
  • Double-entry ledger

A trade is a promise. Settlement is when the money and shares actually move. Indian equities settle on T+1: a trade on Monday settles on Tuesday.

  • Positions vs holdings: a share bought today is a position. After settlement it arrives in the user's demat account at the depository and becomes a holding. Until then the app shows it as "T1".
  • End of day: the exchange sends a trade file. The broker matches it line by line against its own fills; any difference is a break that someone must explain before settlement. Contract notes go to every user who traded.
  • Obligations: the clearing corporation nets each broker's trades into one amount of money and a quantity of each stock to deliver. The broker pays in funds for its buyers and delivers shares from its sellers' demat accounts. Selling a holding needs the user's authorisation at the depository.
  • Funds ledger: every cash movement (deposit, blocked margin, trade value, charges, payout) is a double-entry ledger row. A user's available balance is derived from the ledger, never stored as one number that code updates in place.
  • Corporate actions: splits, bonuses and dividends change holdings overnight. They run as batch jobs before the next open, with the same reconciliation.

"The broker checks and routes orders; the exchange matches them. An order goes through a gateway, an order service that saves it with an idempotency key, and a risk system that blocks margin. The risk system is partitioned by account with one thread each, so margin can't be double spent. The exchange gateway sends orders within the exchange's rate cap and never resends from the SENT state; it reconciles instead. Fills come back through Kafka to positions, the ledger and a push to the app. Prices are a separate path: a ticker plant conflates the exchange feed to the latest value per symbol, and WebSocket gateways batch one frame a second per phone, dropping old ticks for slow clients. For 9:15 we scale on a schedule, queue overnight orders fairly, and put exits ahead of new orders."
