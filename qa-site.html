Classic system design walkthrough

Q&A Site

Design a question and answer site like Quora: people ask questions, others answer, readers comment and vote, and the best answer should rise to the top. Each person gets a feed of questions and answers from the topics and people they follow. Stop the same question from being asked a thousand times, make everything searchable, and keep spam out. Plan for 100 million daily users.

Requirements

  • Clarify
  • Non-functional requirements

A Q&A site is a read-heavy content site with three hard parts: putting the best answer first, building a feed per person, and keeping one question in one place.

QuestionAssumptionWhy it matters
FeaturesAsk, answer, comment, upvote and downvote, follow topics and people, feed, searchWrite path, read path and feed are separate
Users300M a month, 100M a dayPage and feed load
New content500k questions, 2M answers, 5M comments a daySmall write load; long-lived content
Votes100M a dayThe busiest write; drives ranking
Reads~10 question pages and ~3 feed loads per user a dayCaching decides cost
Latencyquestion page p99 under 300 ms; feed under 500 msPrecompute what you can
Freshnessnew answer visible in seconds; search in under a minuteEvents, not batch jobs

Out of scope: payments to writers, ads, private messages and live sessions. Most traffic arrives from web search engines landing on a question page, so that page must be fast for logged-out readers too.

Estimates

  • Back of the envelope
  • Numbers
  • Reads: 100M users × (10 pages + 3 feeds) = 1.3B a day ≈ 15k a second, maybe 45k at the evening peak. Cache hit rates of 90%+ on question pages are normal, because popular questions get most views.
  • Content writes: 500k questions + 2M answers = 2.5M a day ≈ 29 a second. Add 5M comments for about 87 a second. One database could take this; we shard for size and reads, not write rate.
  • Votes: 100M a day ≈ 1,160 a second on average. A viral answer can get thousands a minute, so one counter row would be a hot spot.
  • Storage: 400M questions at ~0.5 KB ≈ 200 GB; 1.5B answers at ~2 KB ≈ 3 TB; votes at ~25 bytes a row add ~2.5 GB a day. Images live in a blob store behind a CDN.
  • Embeddings for duplicates: 400M × 768 dimensions × 4 bytes ≈ 1.2 TB as floats, 300 GB as 8-bit integers, or ~26 GB with 64-byte product quantization codes. That choice decides whether the vector index fits in memory.

The vote is a PUT of the final state, not "add one". Sending it twice, or from two tabs, leaves the same single vote. 0 removes it. The similar-questions call runs while the user types, before the question exists.

Architecture

  • Whole system

Questions, answers, comments

  • Deep dive 1
  • Databases
  • Unique IDs
  • Caching

Shard everything on a question page by the question's id. Then rendering a page is one shard lookup, not a scatter across the cluster.

questions(question_id PK, title, body, author_id, topic_ids, canonical_id NULL, status, created_at)
answers  (question_id, answer_id, author_id, body, up, down, score, status, created_at)
           PRIMARY KEY (question_id, answer_id)
comments (question_id, answer_id, comment_id, parent_id NULL, author_id, body, created_at)
votes    (question_id, answer_id, user_id, value)  PRIMARY KEY (answer_id, user_id)
answers_by_author(author_id, answer_id, question_id)   -- separate table, sharded by author_id
  • IDs: Snowflake-style 64-bit ids, so rows sort by time and the shard can be found from the question id alone.
  • Comments as a tree: a parent_id gives replies to replies. Load the first 3 top-level comments with the page and fetch the rest on click; most readers never open them.
  • Profiles: "all answers by this person" uses answers_by_author, sharded by author and filled from the same event, so it never needs a cross-shard query.
  • Edits: answers are edited often. Keep the current body in the row and old versions in an append-only history table, and invalidate the page cache on each edit.
  • The page cache: store the ranked list of answer ids per question and each rendered answer body separately. A vote changes the order list; an edit changes one body. Neither throws away the whole page.

Votes and counters

  • Deep dive 2
  • Hot partitions
  • Queues

A vote has two jobs: remember that this person voted, exactly once, and move a counter that thousands of others are moving too.

  1. Record the vote: upsert votes(answer_id, user_id) = value. The primary key makes a double vote impossible; the old value tells you the change (up to down is −1 up and +1 down).
  2. Publish the change: the vote service writes a VoteChanged event (through an outbox, so it is never lost) with the deltas, not the totals.
  3. Count in batches: a consumer adds the deltas to Redis counters right away (so the number on screen moves), and flushes summed deltas to the answers.up and down columns every few seconds. One write per answer per flush, however many votes arrived.
  4. Viral answers: if one answer gets thousands of votes a minute, split its Redis counter into N sub-keys and sum them on read, as on the hot partitions page.
  5. "Did I vote?": the page needs your votes on, say, 20 answers. Because votes carry question_id and live on that question's shard, that is one indexed query.

Counters can drift if a flush is lost or replayed. A nightly job recounts from the votes table, which is the truth, and fixes any answer that is off.

Answer ranking

  • Deep dive 3
  • Feed ranking

Sorting by upvotes minus downvotes buries a great new answer under an old famous one. Sorting by the share of upvotes puts a 1-for-1 answer above a 950-for-1,000 answer. Neither is what readers want.

The fix is to ask: given these votes, what share of readers do we confidently think like this answer? The Wilson score interval answers that. Take its lower bound: the share of upvotes we are 95% sure the answer is at least as good as.

p = up / n, n = up + down, z = 1.96 (95%)
wilson = ( p + z²/2n − z·√( p(1−p)/n + z²/4n² ) ) / ( 1 + z²/n )
  • Few votes, low score: 1 up and 0 down gives about 0.21, not 1.0. The answer has to earn more votes before it can sit at the top.
  • Many votes, close to the true share: 950 up and 50 down gives about 0.93, just under its 95% raw share.
  • Cheap to compute: two counters per answer, so the score is recomputed on every counter flush and stored in answers.score.

Real ranking mixes this with other signals: the author's track record in the topic, whether readers expand and finish the answer, its length and formatting, reports, and how new it is. A learned model can combine them, but Wilson is the honest base that stops tiny samples from winning.

    The personalized feed

    • Deep dive 4
    • News feed
    • Caching

    A Q&A feed is not a timeline of friends' posts. It mixes good answers in topics you follow, questions you could answer, and things people you follow wrote or upvoted. Age matters much less: a great answer from 2019 is still a great read.

    • Candidate generation: pull a few hundred items from several sources: top-scored recent answers in each followed topic, new answers by followed people, questions in your areas with few answers ("answer requests"), and what is trending. Topics have millions of followers, so this is pull at read time, not fan-out on write.
    • Per-topic pools: a stream job keeps a list of the best recent answer ids per topic in Redis, updated from vote and answer events. Reading 30 followed topics is then 30 small list reads, not 30 database queries.
    • Ranking: a model predicts, per candidate, the chance you will read, expand, upvote or answer it, using topic affinity, the author, the answer's score and how fresh it is. Keep the top 50.
    • Filtering: drop what you have already seen (a per-user set of recent ids), what you muted, and anything the spam models flagged. Mix in a few new topics so the feed doesn't narrow forever.
    • Precompute for active users: build the next page in the background after each load, so the scroll feels instant. Users who return after weeks get a fresh build.

    Duplicate questions

    • Deep dive 5
    • Near-duplicate pages

    "How do I learn Python fast?" and "Quickest way to learn Python?" should be one page with all the good answers, not two half-answered ones. Catch it in two places: while the person types, and after the question is posted.

    • While typing: after a short pause, run two searches in parallel: a word search (BM25) on question titles, and a nearest-neighbour search on an embedding of the text. Merge the top 50, rerank them with a small model that reads both questions together, and show the best 3 to 5 with "Your question may already have an answer".
    • Vector index: an approximate nearest-neighbour index such as HNSW or IVF-PQ over 400M question vectors. Quantized to 64 bytes a vector it is ~26 GB, so a few shards keep it in memory. Results come back in tens of milliseconds.
    • After posting: a consumer re-runs the check with a bigger model. Scores above a high bar (say 0.95) go to a merge queue; between 0.85 and 0.95, a moderator or trusted user decides. Below that, nothing happens.
    • Merging: set canonical_id on the duplicate, move its answers and followers to the canonical question, and make the old URL a 301 redirect, so links and search rankings follow.
    • Precision over recall: a wrong merge hides a different question and annoys two sets of people. A missed duplicate only costs some extra answers. Tune for few false merges.

    Search

    • Deep dive 6
    • Indexes
    • Index: Elasticsearch with an inverted index over question titles (weighted most), question bodies, answer text and topic names. Shard by document id; replicate for read load.
    • Freshness: MySQL changes flow through CDC into Kafka and an indexer, so a new question is searchable within seconds to a minute.
    • Ranking: text relevance first, then quality signals: answer count, best answer score, views and whether it is a canonical question. Duplicates are hidden behind their canonical page.
    • Typeahead: a separate prefix index over the few million most-viewed question titles, served from memory, answers as you type in under 50 ms.
    • Semantic search: the same embeddings built for duplicates also catch searches whose words differ from the question's, so one vector index serves both jobs.

    Spam and quality

    • Governance
    • Rate limiting
    • Live chat moderation

    Open sites attract link spam, copied answers, voting rings and low-effort replies. Layer cheap checks first and slow ones later.

    LayerSignalAction
    At the edgeRequests per account, IP and device; new account ageRate limits; tighter limits for new accounts
    On writeLink domains with bad history; text classifier; same text posted many times (SimHash)Block, or hold for review
    After writeReports, downvotes, readers who leave fastCollapse the answer, lower its rank
    VotesGroups of new accounts that always upvote the same author; shared IPs or devicesDiscount those votes in the score; do not tell the ring
    AuthorsTrack record per topic: how their past answers were ratedA trust weight on new answers and on their votes

    Discounting votes quietly works better than deleting them: the voter still sees their vote, but the ranking ignores it, so the ring learns nothing about what was caught.

    Presenting it in 45 minutes

    • Interview script
    StepTimeWhat to cover
    1. Clarify5 minFeatures, scale, read vs write mix, what "best answer" means
    2. Estimate3 min15k reads/s, 29 writes/s, 1.2k votes/s, 3 TB of text
    3. API and data model5 minVote as PUT of state; everything for a page sharded by question
    4. Draw the whole system7 minPage cache, Q&A and vote services, MySQL, Kafka, Redis, search
    5. Deep dive: votes and ranking10 minOne vote per user, batched counters, Wilson lower bound
    6. Deep dive: feed or duplicates10 minCandidates and ranking; or BM25 + embeddings, merge with canonical id
    7. Risks and v25 minSpam and rings, wrong merges, hot counters

    "This is a read-heavy content site, about 500 reads for every new question or answer. Everything on a question page lives on one MySQL shard keyed by question id, and the page is served from a cache of answer ids and rendered bodies. A vote is an upsert keyed by answer and user, so it counts once; deltas go through Kafka and are batched into counters. Answers are ordered by the Wilson lower bound so a tiny sample can't win. The feed pulls candidates from per-topic pools in Redis and ranks them with a model. Duplicates are caught while typing with word search plus embeddings, and after posting with a merge queue that sets a canonical id and redirects the old URL."
