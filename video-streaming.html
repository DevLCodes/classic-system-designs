Video Streaming

Design a video platform like YouTube or Netflix. Creators upload videos; viewers search, press play and watch on phones, laptops and TVs over networks from fibre to patchy mobile. Plan for 500 million daily viewers and 500 hours of new video uploaded every minute.

Requirements

  • Clarify
  • Non-functional requirements

Video looks like a storage problem, but it is mostly a bandwidth problem. The design is shaped by three constraints: network bandwidth, the variety of devices, and the variety of formats they play.

Out of scope: live streaming (a different latency problem), ads, payments and offline downloads. YouTube-style open uploads and Netflix-style curated catalogues share the same playback design; the difference is upload volume and moderation.

Estimates

  • Back of the envelope
  • Numbers
  • Concurrency: 500M viewers × 30 min = 15 billion viewer-minutes a day. Divided by 1,440 minutes, that is about 10 million streams at any moment on average, and about 20 million in the evening peak.
  • Egress: 10M streams × ~2.5 Mb/s (a mix of phone and TV qualities) ≈ 26 Tb/s on average, 50+ Tb/s at peak. No single data centre serves that; it has to come from thousands of caches near viewers.
  • Upload storage: 500 hours a minute is 30,000 minutes of video a minute. One compressed copy at ~6 MB a minute is 180 GB a minute, about 95 PB a year. The full ladder of renditions is roughly 4× that, so about 1 PB of new encoded video a day. Originals go to cheaper cold storage.
  • Requests: 10M streams each fetching a 4-second segment ≈ 2.6M segment requests a second, nearly all answered by caches. The API sees far less: about 3B plays a day ≈ 35k manifest requests a second.

Uploads use presigned URLs so large files never pass through our API servers, and multipart, resumable uploads so a dropped connection resumes from the last part instead of starting again. The play call returns a manifest URL already pointing at a specific cache chosen for this viewer, with a signed, expiring token.

Architecture

  • Whole system
  • Upload path: clients write straight to object storage; a completion event on Kafka starts processing.
  • Processing pipeline: checks, splits and transcodes the video into many renditions, makes thumbnails and writes everything to the origin store.
  • Control plane: stateless services behind an API gateway (Netflix's Zuul is one) that decide what a viewer may play and where to fetch it from. Netflix runs this in AWS.
  • Data plane: a CDN of caches near viewers: inside ISPs where they agree, at internet exchange points (IXPs) where they don't, backed by the origin. Netflix built its own, Open Connect; YouTube uses Google's edge network.

Upload and processing

  • Deep dive 1
  • Queues

A two-hour 4K film is too big to transcode as one job. Split it into chunks, process the chunks in parallel, and assemble the results.

  1. Validate. Probe the file (container, codecs, duration). Reject what can't be decoded.
  2. Content checks. Fingerprint against known copyrighted material (YouTube's Content ID works this way) and run classifiers for nudity and violence. Flagged videos stay private pending review.
  3. Chunk. Cut the source at keyframes into pieces of a few seconds to a minute.
  4. Transcode in parallel. Each chunk is encoded into every rendition of the bitrate ladder, for example 240p at 0.4 Mb/s up to 1080p at 5 Mb/s and 4K at 15 Mb/s, in H.264 for every device plus VP9 or AV1 for those that support them (30–50% smaller at the same quality). Thousands of workers pull chunk jobs from a queue; a failed chunk is simply retried.
  5. Package. Re-cut each rendition into 2–6 second segments and write manifests for HLS (.m3u8) and DASH (.mpd). Newer setups package once with CMAF and serve both.
  6. Thumbnails and tags. Pick candidate frames, detect language and topics for search and recommendations.
  7. Publish. Write segments to the origin, flip the video to ready in metadata, and emit an event so search, notifications and feeds pick it up.

The pipeline is a DAG of tasks run by a workflow engine, so a 10-minute video becomes a few hundred small jobs and is playable within minutes. Popular videos get extra work later: Netflix encodes each title per shot with tuned settings, because spending more compute once saves bandwidth on every view.

Make it idempotent. Every task writes to a deterministic path (/v/{id}/{rendition}/{segment}.m4s), so a retried or duplicated job overwrites the same output instead of creating a second copy. The upload-complete event can be delivered twice without harm.

Adaptive playback

  • Deep dive 2

The player, not the server, decides the quality. It fetches a manifest that lists every rendition, then asks for each next segment in whichever quality the network can sustain right now.

HLSDASH
OriginAppleOpen standard (MPEG)
Manifest.m3u8 text playlist.mpd XML
SegmentsTraditionally .ts, now fMP4/CMAF; often 6 sfMP4; flexible, often 2–4 s
Native supportiOS, Safari, most TVsAndroid, browsers via Media Source Extensions

Both are plain HTTP, which is the point: segments are ordinary files that any HTTP cache can store and serve, with no long-lived streaming connections to keep alive. That is why they replaced RTMP.

How the player picks a quality

  • Throughput-based: measure how fast recent segments downloaded and pick the highest rendition below that, with a safety margin.
  • Buffer-based: look at how many seconds are buffered. A full buffer allows a higher quality; a draining buffer forces a lower one. Netflix found buffer-based rules cut rebuffering noticeably.
  • Fast start: start at a low rendition so the first frame appears quickly, then climb.

Shorter segments react faster and start faster but mean more requests and slightly worse compression. Four seconds is a common middle ground for on-demand video.

Delivering the bytes

  • Deep dive 3
  • Caching
  • DNS

At 50 Tb/s the only affordable byte is one that never crosses the internet backbone. So the caches go where the viewers are: inside their ISP.

  1. ISP caches. The platform gives ISPs cache servers to place in their own networks (Netflix's Open Connect appliances; Google's Global Cache for YouTube). The ISP saves transit costs, viewers get bytes from a few hops away. Netflix serves nearly all its traffic this way.
  2. IXP caches. Where an ISP won't host servers, caches sit at internet exchange points, where many networks connect. They also fill the ISP caches.
  3. Origin. Object storage in a few regions holds everything. Only misses for long-tail videos reach it.

Push the head, pull the tail

Viewing is extremely skewed: a small share of titles takes most of the views. For a catalogue like Netflix's, the system predicts what each region will watch tomorrow and pushes it into the caches overnight, when links are idle. YouTube's open catalogue is too large to predict fully, so caches mostly pull on a miss and keep what is popular (LRU or smarter policies).

Steering

The playback service chooses the cache for each play, using the viewer's network, cache health and load, and returns it in the manifest URL with fallbacks. That's more precise than DNS-based routing, and lets the player switch caches mid-stream if one fails.

View counts are a classic trap: a viral video takes thousands of increments a second on one row. Count in memory per server or in a stream job and write aggregated deltas, and accept that the displayed count lags by seconds.

Search, comments and recommendations

  • Kafka

All of these hang off the event stream rather than the request path. When a video is published, a search consumer indexes it in Elasticsearch, a notification service tells subscribers, and the recommendation system adds it as a candidate. Playback events (start, quality switches, rebuffers, progress every few seconds) flow through Kafka into a stream processor for real-time quality dashboards and into a data lake for training recommendation models.

The home page is itself a service: it asks the recommendation system for ranked rows, fills them with metadata and thumbnails, and caches the result per user for minutes. Most plays start from there, not from search.

Deployment and resilience

  • Production

The control plane runs in at least three regions, active-active, with load balanced first across zones and then across instances. Each service call has a timeout, a retry budget and a circuit breaker (Netflix's Hystrix popularized the pattern): if the recommendations service is slow, the home page shows a cached or generic row instead of failing.

Netflix famously tests this in production: Chaos Monkey kills instances, and region evacuation drills move all traffic out of a region. The data plane is naturally resilient: thousands of caches, and players hold several fallback URLs.

Degrade gracefully: if the control plane is struggling, playback of what's already started must continue, because segments come from caches with tokens valid for hours. Losing the "continue watching" row is acceptable; stopping a film mid-scene is not.

  • Wrap-up

v2 ideas: live streaming with low-latency HLS, offline downloads, per-shot encoding for the whole catalogue, and AV1 everywhere hardware allows.

"The defining number is egress: 10 million concurrent streams at about 2.5 Mb/s is 26 terabits a second, so the design is mostly about where bytes come from. Creators upload with presigned multipart URLs straight to object storage; an event starts a pipeline that chunks the video and transcodes each chunk in parallel into a bitrate ladder, packaged as HLS and DASH segments. Playback has a small control plane in the cloud that checks rights and picks a cache, and a data plane of caches inside ISPs that serve 95% of bytes. The player adapts quality per segment from its buffer. Popular titles are pushed to caches overnight; the long tail is pulled on a miss."
