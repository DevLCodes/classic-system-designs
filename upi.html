Classic system design walkthrough

UPI Payments Switch

Design the central switch behind India's UPI: a user types a VPA such as asha@okbank, enters a PIN, and money moves from their bank account to someone else's in a few seconds, any time of day. Hundreds of banks and dozens of payment apps connect to it. Plan for 700 million payments a day, peaks of 25,000 a second, and banks whose core systems are sometimes slow or down. No payment may ever be debited twice, and no debit may be left without a credit or a refund.

Requirements

  • Clarify
  • Non-functional requirements

UPI is not a wallet. Money stays in bank accounts the whole time. The switch is a referee that tells one bank to take money out and another to put it in, and remembers exactly what happened.

QuestionAssumptionWhy it matters
Who connects?Payment apps (PSPs) on one side, banks on the other, both through the switchThe switch talks to hundreds of systems it doesn't control
Which payments?Push (pay a VPA or QR), pull (collect request), person to person and person to merchantSame core flow, different starting side
How do users name each other?A VPA like ravi@ybl, a phone number or a QR code; never an account numberThe switch must turn a name into a bank account on every payment
Scale?700M payments a day, 25k a second at peakEvery payment touches two banks, so bank capacity matters more than switch capacity
Latency?Most payments done in 2 to 5 seconds; hard timeout per request of about 15 secondsThe user is staring at a spinner at a shop counter
Money rules?No double debits; a debit without a credit must be credited or refunded within a dayThe regulator sets deadlines and penalties for stuck money
Hours?24 × 7, including bank holidays and while banks run their nightly batch jobsBanks go into maintenance; the switch can't

Out of scope: the payment apps' own features (offers, bill pay), card networks, and fraud models inside banks. General card and wallet payments have their own walkthrough: Payments.

The last line is the real constraint. The switch can scale out across machines; a bank's core banking system often can't, and it is the slowest hop in every payment.

API

  • Interface
  • Protocols

Real UPI messages are signed XML over HTTPS between registered parties. The shape, simplified:

PSP → switch     ReqPay      { txnId, payer: asha@okbank, payee: ravi@ybl, amount: 640.00,
                               cred: <PIN encrypted on the phone>, device, note }
switch → PSP     ReqAuthDetails { txnId, payee: ravi@ybl }   → { account, ifsc, name }
switch → bank    ReqPay DEBIT   { txnId, rrn, account, amount, cred } → SUCCESS | FAILURE (reason)
switch → bank    ReqPay CREDIT  { txnId, rrn, account, amount }       → SUCCESS | FAILURE
switch → PSPs    RespPay / confirmation { txnId, rrn, status }
anyone → switch  ReqChkTxn   { txnId }  → SUCCESS | FAILURE | DEEMED | PENDING
switch → bank    ReqPay REVERSAL { txnId, rrn, original: DEBIT } → SUCCESS | FAILURE

Every call is asynchronous: the receiver acknowledges at once, then sends the real response as a separate message when it's done. The txnId is created once by the payer's PSP and travels on every message, so every party can match, retry and deduplicate by it. The switch adds a 12-digit RRN (retrieval reference number) that users and support staff quote.

Architecture

  • Whole system
  • Payer PSP: the app plus the bank that sponsors it. It creates the txnId, collects the PIN through a secure library that encrypts it on the phone, and starts the payment.
  • Payee PSP: owns the handle after the @ (for example @ybl) and maps each VPA to a real account.
  • Remitter bank (the payer's bank, sometimes called the issuer): decrypts and checks the PIN, checks balance and limits, debits.
  • Beneficiary bank (the payee's bank; for merchants the acquiring side): credits the account.
  • The switch: routes, runs a state machine per payment, enforces timeouts, starts reversals, and produces settlement files. It never sees the PIN in clear and never holds money.

VPA resolution

  • Deep dive 1
  • DNS
  • Caching

A VPA works like an email address: the part after the @ says who to ask, and only that party knows the answer.

  1. Handle to PSP: the switch keeps a small table of handles (@ybl, @okbank, …) to PSP endpoints. A few hundred rows, held in memory on every switch node, changed by an admin process.
  2. VPA to account: the switch sends ReqAuthDetails to the payee's PSP, which looks up ravi@ybl and returns the account number, IFSC (bank and branch code) and the registered name.
  3. IFSC to bank: the first four letters of the IFSC name the bank, so the switch knows where to send the credit.
  4. Show the name: the payer's app shows "Ravi Kumar" before the PIN screen. This one step stops most wrong-person payments.

Why not cache VPA to account at the switch? Users move a VPA to a new account, and a stale entry sends money to the old one. Caching the handle table is safe; caching the answer is not. QR codes for merchants usually carry the VPA, so the same lookup runs on every scan.

Debit, then credit

  • Deep dive 2
  • Sagas

There is no transaction that spans two banks. The switch runs a two-step saga: take the money out first, then put it in, and undo the first step if the second one fails.

StateEntered whenNext
INITIATEDReqPay accepted, txnId storedResolve payee
RESOLVEDPayee PSP returned an accountSend debit
DEBIT_PENDINGDebit sent to remitter bank, timer startedDEBITED, FAILED (wrong PIN, no balance) or debit timeout
DEBITEDRemitter bank said SUCCESSSend credit
CREDIT_PENDINGCredit sent to beneficiary bank, timer startedSUCCESS, credit failure or credit timeout
SUCCESSBeneficiary bank said SUCCESSNotify both PSPs; done
DEEMEDCredit timed out after a successful debitResolved by check status or reconciliation
REVERSAL_PENDING → REVERSEDDebit unknown or credit failedMoney back to payer; done

Why debit first? If you credited first and the debit then failed, you would have handed out money you can't get back. A failed credit leaves money at the payer's own bank, which can always give it back. Each transition is a conditional write on the txnId row (UPDATE … WHERE state = 'DEBIT_PENDING'), so a late or duplicate response can't move a payment backwards.

Timeouts, deemed success and reversals

  • Deep dive 3
  • Payments
  • Retries

A timeout is not a failure. It means "I don't know". The design is mostly about what to do when you don't know which leg happened.

What went wrongWhere the money isWhat the switch doesUser sees
Debit declined (wrong PIN, no balance)Still with payerMark FAILED, notifyFailed, with reason
Debit timed outUnknown: maybe debitedSend a reversal to the remitter bank; the bank undoes the debit if it happened, or answers "no such debit"Failed; any debit comes back
Credit declined (account closed or frozen)Debited from payer, at the remitter bankSend a reversal: credit the payer backFailed, refunded in seconds
Credit timed outDebited; maybe creditedMark DEEMED; chase with check status; reconciliation settles itPending
  • Why not reverse a timed-out credit? The payee may already have the money. If you reverse the debit too, the system has created money. So a credit timeout is treated as a success for settlement (deemed approved), and the beneficiary bank must either confirm the credit or return the money, by T+1.
  • Check status: the switch and PSPs ask "what happened to txnId?" a few times, a few seconds apart, with backoff. Most pending payments resolve within a minute this way.
  • Deadlines: the regulator expects a debited-but-not-credited payment to be fixed by the next day, with compensation to the customer for each day of delay after that. That is why every timer must end in an action.
  • Shorter timeouts help: waiting 30 seconds for a dead bank ties up capacity and the user. Response time limits were cut in 2025 so a slow bank fails fast and the user can retry another way. The lab below shows the trade-off.

Idempotency

  • Deep dive 4
  • Exactly-once

Messages get lost and retried at every hop. The only safe design is one where doing a step twice has the same effect as doing it once.

  • One id, end to end: the payer's PSP creates the txnId before the first call. A user tapping "Pay" twice, or the app retrying after a network drop, sends the same id.
  • Switch: INSERT of the transaction row with txnId as the primary key. A second ReqPay with the same id gets the current status back, not a second payment.
  • Banks: each bank keeps the txnId (or RRN) with the debit or credit in the same database transaction as the balance change. A repeated debit request returns the first result. This is what makes check status and retries safe.
  • Reversals: keyed by the original txnId too, so a reversal sent twice refunds once, and a reversal for a debit that never happened is a harmless "nothing to reverse".
  • New attempt, new id: if the user gives up and pays again, that is a new txnId. The app should show the first one as pending, so users don't pay twice by hand.

Settlement and reconciliation

  • Proof
  • Ledger

The messages move in seconds. The money between banks moves later, in a few large net amounts.

  1. Cycles: the day is cut into several settlement cycles. For each cycle the switch adds up, per bank, everything it owes and is owed. If Bank A's customers paid Bank B's customers ₹900 crore and B's paid A's ₹850 crore, only the ₹50 crore difference moves.
  2. Settle: the net positions are posted to the banks' settlement accounts at the central bank. Thousands of crores in millions of payments become a few hundred transfers.
  3. Files: each bank gets a raw file of every transaction in the cycle with its final status (success, deemed, reversed).
  4. Match: each bank matches the file against its core banking records by txnId or RRN. A deemed payment it never credited must now be credited or returned; a debit with no matching record must be reversed.
  5. Disputes: anything still unmatched becomes an adjustment or a complaint, with deadlines and penalties. Reconciliation is the safety net that catches whatever every timer and retry missed.

Deployment and resilience

  • Production
  • Circuit breakers
  • Rate limiting
  • Active-active data centres: at least two sites in different cities, both taking traffic. Each txnId belongs to one site so its state machine has one owner; the other site holds a synchronous copy and takes over on failure.
  • Per-bank circuit breakers: when a bank's timeouts jump, stop sending it new payments for a short while and fail them fast with a clear "bank unavailable" reason. Waiting 15 seconds on a dead bank just builds up open transactions.
  • Per-bank rate limits: send each bank no more than the throughput it has declared, so one festival spike doesn't knock its core system over.
  • Planned downtime: banks announce maintenance windows, and payments to or from them are declined up front during the window instead of timing out.
  • Security: mutual TLS and signed messages between all parties, PIN encrypted on the device for the issuer only, device binding of the user's phone number, and limits per transaction and per day.

"The switch never holds money; it runs a state machine per payment, keyed by a txnId the payer's app creates. It resolves the payee's VPA through the payee's PSP, then runs a two-step saga: debit the payer's bank first, then credit the payee's bank. Every leg has a timer. A debit timeout triggers a reversal, because undoing a debit that may not have happened is safe. A credit timeout becomes a deemed success, because the payee may already have the money; we chase it with check status and settle it in reconciliation by T+1. Every bank dedupes on the txnId, so retries and check status are safe. Money between banks settles later in net amounts per cycle, and reconciliation against those files is the final safety net."

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each question to compare.

The credit leg to the payee's bank times out after a successful debit. What should the switch do?

Mark the payment DEEMED, chase it with check status, and settle by T+1. Reversing could create money if the payee was actually credited. Read the section

A user's app retries ReqPay after a network drop. How is a double debit avoided?

The same txnId is reused, and the switch and banks dedupe on it. One id end to end makes retries idempotent. Read the section

One large bank's core system slows down during a festival, and open transactions across the switch climb. What should happen?

Trip a per-bank circuit breaker and fail fast, with per-bank rate limits. Waiting on a dead bank only builds open transactions. Read the section

A user moved ravi@ybl to a new bank account yesterday, but a payment was credited to the old account. What design mistake causes this?

Caching VPA to account answers at the switch. The VPA lookup must run against the payee PSP on every payment. Read the section
