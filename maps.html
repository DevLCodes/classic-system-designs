Maps and Navigation

Design a maps app like Google Maps: people pan and zoom a map of the world, search for an address, get driving directions with an arrival time, and get a faster route when traffic changes. Plan for 100 million daily users, 50 million route requests a day, and 5 million people navigating at once at the evening peak.

Requirements

  • Clarify
  • Non-functional requirements

A maps app is three products glued together: a picture of the world that rarely changes, a shortest-path search that must answer in milliseconds, and a live feed of how fast every road is moving right now.

Estimates

  • Back of the envelope
  • Numbers
  • Tiles: 100M users × ~100 tiles a day = 10B tile requests a day ≈ 116k a second, ~350k at the evening peak. Tiles never change within a map version, so phones and the CDN serve almost all of them.
  • Search: 100M × ~3 searches = 300M a day ≈ 3.5k a second, ~10k at peak, plus autocomplete on every few keystrokes.
  • Routes: 50M a day ≈ 580 a second, ~2k at peak. Each request finds about three alternatives, and reroutes add more.
  • Pings: 5M trips sending a location every 5 s (batches of 1-second GPS fixes) = 1M requests a second. At ~100 bytes per fix that is 100 MB/s at peak, or about 3 TB a day at an average of a third of peak.
  • Road graph: hundreds of millions of junctions and over a billion road segments worldwide. One continent fits in the memory of one large server (tens of GB), so routing servers are replicated per region rather than sharded per query.

The tile URL carries the map version, so a tile is never updated in place: a new map version is a new URL, and old tiles simply age out of caches. The answer to each location batch carries the fresh ETA and any faster-route offer, so navigation needs no separate push channel while the app is open.

Architecture

  • Whole system

Map tiles and zoom levels

  • Deep dive 1
  • CDN
  • Caching

Nobody draws the world on request. The map is cut into square tiles at every zoom level ahead of time, stored as files, and served from caches like any other static asset.

  • Naming: a tile is z/x/y. Zoom z has 2z × 2z = 4z tiles. Central Bengaluru at zoom 14 is tile 14/11723/7596. The phone works out which tiles cover the screen with simple arithmetic; no server lookup is needed.
  • Vector, not pictures: modern maps send vector tiles (roads, buildings and labels as geometry, a few KB each) and draw them on the phone's GPU. One tile set serves every style (day, night, satellite overlay), every language, and smooth rotation and 3D tilt. Raster tiles (PNG pictures) need a full copy per style and per language.
  • Generalisation: each zoom level keeps only what is visible at that scale. Zoom 5 has motorways and big cities; zoom 14 has every street. Shapes are simplified per level so a country's outline doesn't carry every bend of its coastline.
  • Overzoom: vector tiles stop at about zoom 14–16. When you zoom further, the phone keeps scaling the zoom-14 geometry, which is precise to well under a metre. That avoids storing the billions of tiles at zooms 15–20.
  • Empty tiles: about 70% of the planet is ocean. All empty tiles point to one shared file, so storage grows with land, not area.
  • Caching: tiles are immutable per map version, so the CDN caches them for a year and phones keep thousands on disk. A new version changes only tiles whose data changed; the rest are copied forward and stay warm in caches.

Format

Rough model: every tile down to zoom 5 is stored; from zoom 6 only the share with content is stored, and empty tiles share one file. Average sizes are compressed and include sparse rural tiles; a dense city-centre vector tile can be 50–100 KB. 1 GB = 109 bytes.

Search and geocoding

  • Deep dive 2
  • Nearby places
  • Indexes

Geocoding turns text into a point: "12 MG Road, Bengaluru" becomes 12.9757, 77.6062. Reverse geocoding turns a point into the nearest address.

  1. Parse: split the query into parts (house number, street, locality, city, postcode). Address formats differ by country, so the parser is trained per region; "12 MG Road" and "MG Road 12" must mean the same thing.
  2. Match: look the parts up in a search index of streets, address points and named places (Elasticsearch or a custom inverted index), with spelling tolerance, abbreviations ("Rd" = "Road") and local names in several scripts.
  3. Rank: combine text match, distance from the user's map view and place popularity. "Starbucks" should mean the one nearby, not the most famous one.
  4. Interpolate: if house 12 has no exact point, estimate it along the street segment between known numbers 2 and 40, on the even side.
  5. Reverse: a point lookup in a geo index (S2 or geohash cells) of address points and road segments, then pick the nearest. This is the same index shape as Nearby places.

Search is read-only and shards cleanly by region, so it scales with replicas. Autocomplete answers from a prefix index with heavy caching, because millions of people type the same first three letters.

Routing on a road graph

  • Deep dive 3
  • Ride matching ETAs

Roads are a directed graph: junctions are nodes, road segments are edges, and each edge's weight is the time to drive it. Directions are a shortest-path search on that graph.

  • The graph: each edge stores length, speed limit, road class, and whether it is one-way. Turn restrictions ("no right turn here") and turn costs (a left across traffic takes longer) are modelled by putting the weight on pairs of edges, not just edges.
  • Dijkstra: settle nodes in order of travel time from the start until the destination is settled. Correct, but it grows a circle in every direction: a 300 km trip settles millions of nodes and takes seconds.
  • A*: add an estimate of the time left (straight-line distance ÷ top speed) so the search leans toward the goal. It must never overestimate, or it can miss the best route. It settles a few times fewer nodes, still far too many for a continent.
  • Bidirectional: search forward from the start and backward from the goal at the same time and stop where they meet. Two small circles cover far less area than one big one.

Contraction hierarchies: precompute the shortcuts

  • Why it is fast: long trips always climb to arterials and motorways, so a query settles a few hundred nodes instead of millions and finishes in well under a millisecond. The cost is a preprocessing run of minutes to hours per region and roughly double the edges.
  • The traffic problem: plain contraction hierarchies bake the weights into the shortcuts, so a jam would need a rebuild. Customizable versions (CCH, or multi-level partitions as in CRP) split the work: the order and shape are computed once from the road layout, and new weights are applied in seconds. That is what lets routing follow live traffic.
  • Alternatives: run the search again with the first route's edges penalised, and keep routes that are different enough and not much slower. Three is the usual answer.
  • Serving: each routing server loads a region's graph into memory and answers any query inside it. Long cross-region trips use a top-level graph of the most important roads. Scale by adding replicas behind a load balancer.

Live traffic from phones

  • Deep dive 4
  • Streams
  • Partitioning

Every phone that is navigating is also a traffic sensor. A million pings a second, turned into one speed per road segment, is the live traffic layer.

  1. Ingest: the navigation service accepts batches of GPS fixes and writes them to Kafka, keyed by map region so one city's pings stay together.
  2. Map matching: raw GPS wanders by 5–20 m and jumps between parallel roads. A hidden Markov model picks the sequence of road segments that best explains the whole trail, not each point alone: a car can't teleport from the flyover to the service road below it.
  3. Aggregate: a stream processor (Flink, say) keeps a sliding window of the last few minutes per segment and computes a robust speed (a median, ignoring a car parked at a bus stop).
  4. Privacy: drop the first and last few hundred metres of each trip (they reveal homes), rotate trip ids, and publish a segment's speed only when enough different phones contributed. Raw trails expire quickly.
  5. Publish: write speeds to the live speed store (one small record per segment, kept in memory), redraw traffic overlay tiles every minute or two, and send changes to routing and navigation.
  6. Fill the gaps: a quiet road at 3 am has no probes. Fall back to the historical speed for that segment at that hour and weekday, then to the speed limit.

Pings arrive at 1M a second, but the output is small: a few hundred million segments, most of them unchanged in any given minute. The stream turns a firehose into a trickle of speed updates.

Predicting arrival times

  • Deep dive 5

An ETA is the sum of the travel times of the route's segments, but each time is a forecast for when you will reach that segment, not a reading of now.

  • Blend: for a segment you'll reach in 2 minutes, trust the live speed. For one 40 minutes away, lean on the historical speed for that hour, because today's jam may have cleared by then.
  • Correct: a learned model adjusts the sum for things the graph misses: traffic lights, turn delays, school zones, weather, events. Models that look at neighbouring segments together (graph neural networks) predict how a jam spreads.
  • Search with the forecast: routing uses the same time-dependent weights, so a route that avoids a motorway jam at 6 pm can be chosen even if it is longer.
  • Keep it honest: compare every finished trip's actual time with the ETA given at the start. That error, split by city and trip length, is the metric that matters.
ETA = Σ segments travel_time(segment, arrival time at that segment) + model correction

Rerouting

  • Deep dive 6
  • Off route: the phone compares each fix with the route line. If it is more than ~30–50 m away for a few seconds (a missed turn), it asks for a new route from its position and heading. The phone does this itself, because it knows first.
  • Faster route: the server keeps an index from segment to the active trips that still have it ahead. When a segment's speed changes a lot, it recomputes only those trips. Checking all 5M trips every minute would be 83k route searches a second for nothing.
  • No flapping: offer a new route only if it saves enough (say more than 5 minutes or 10%), and not again for a while after the last offer. Drivers stop trusting an app that changes its mind every junction.
  • Spread the load: if every app sends everyone down the same side road, that road jams. Large systems watch how many trips they assign to small roads and spread them across alternatives.
  • Offline: with no signal, the phone keeps navigating on the route and tiles it already has, and can reroute on a downloaded region graph, without live traffic.

Building the map

  • Batch pipeline
  • Sources: licensed road data, government records, satellite and street-level imagery (signs and lane markings read by computer vision), user edits and reports, and probe trails that reveal new roads no one has mapped yet.
  • Map data store: one versioned database of features (roads, buildings, places) with history, so a bad edit can be found and rolled back.
  • Build: a batch job (Spark or similar) cuts the features into tiles for every zoom level, and separately compiles the routing graph and its contraction order. Only tiles touched by changed features are rebuilt.
  • Release: a new map version is checked (are any motorways disconnected? did a city's road count drop 20%?) before tiles go live and routing servers load the new graph, region by region.

"The map itself is vector tiles cut per zoom level and named z/x/y; they're immutable per version, so the CDN and phones serve almost all of them. Search parses and matches addresses in a regional index. Routing holds each region's road graph in memory with contraction hierarchies, so a query touches a few hundred nodes, and the customizable variant lets new traffic weights apply in seconds. Navigating phones send a batch of locations every five seconds; a stream job map-matches them, computes a speed per segment and writes it to an in-memory store that routing and the ETA model read. When a segment slows down, we recompute only the trips that still cross it and offer a new route if it saves more than a few minutes."
